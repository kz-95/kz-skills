Rules D1 and D3: find the design system first, compose before building

Tokens, theme file, config, component library - design into it, not beside it. A new component only when nothing existing can be reasonably parameterised.

Wrong: a second button, built fresh

Two buttons, one screen, one purpose. The new one is close enough to look deliberate and different enough to look broken - wrong height, wrong radius, a hex that will stay light-mode purple when the theme flips, and no focus ring. Nobody set out to do this; somebody just did not look first.

Right: the component that already exists

One component, parameterised three ways. It inherits the control height, the radius, the focus ring and the theme for free, which is the whole return on finding the system before writing anything.

Rules D4 and D5: match the surrounding UI, and finish it

Open the two nearest screens that already do this and copy what they do; where they are consistent and disagree with this file, they win. And unless it was called a prototype, it ships finished: spacing, alignment, typography, proportions.

Wrong: correct in isolation, wrong in place
4 rows

Every part of this toolbar is defensible on its own. Together they are three heights, three radii and a gap that matches nothing, because each one was decided here rather than read off the screen next door.

Right: read off the neighbours
4 rows

The same toolbar built from what the rest of this page already uses. D5's bar is not a feeling: the layout audit passes at every width, nothing in Appendix C's list of tells is present, and every value here has a figure behind it.

L - Layout, spacing and hierarchy

What the eye is told to do first, and what survives the content.

Rule L1: Hierarchy before decoration

Establish the 1st, 2nd and 3rd read with size, weight and spacing. Colour and effects come after, if at all.

Wrong: everything shouts

Outstanding balance

RM 48,200

Across 12 accounts

Three equal voices. The eye has no entry point, so the tile reads like a paragraph instead of catching the number.

Right: one thing wins

Outstanding balance

RM 48,200

Across 12 accounts

Size and weight alone do the work, leaving the accent colour free for something that genuinely needs attention.

Rule L5: Controls in a row share one height

Buttons, inputs, selects and chips on the same line come from a single control-height token. Set height, not vertical padding.

Wrong: three heights, one row
SearchClear5 rows

26px, 38px and 20px. Each looks fine alone; together the ragged top edge is the first thing the eye reads, before any label.

Right: one token
5 rows

All three are height: var(--ctl) with align-items: center. Only the height is fixed; padding still comes from the spacing scale.

Rule L7: Layout must survive content

Long names, 0 items, 10,000 items, translated strings. Not just the mock data.

Wrong: built for the mock row
Pusat Perkhidmatan Penyelenggaraan Bangunan Sdn Bhd (Cawangan Utara)
RM 1,250,000.00

The real customer name pushes the amount out of view and adds a horizontal scrollbar. The mock data said "Acme".

Right: one side yields
Pusat Perkhidmatan Penyelenggaraan Bangunan Sdn Bhd (Cawangan Utara)
RM 1,250,000.00

The name truncates with the full value in title; the number never wraps and uses tabular figures so stacked rows align.

Rule L3: spacing is a rhythm, not an interval

Related things sit closer together than unrelated ones, and a heading takes more space above it than below it because it belongs to what follows. One value repeated everywhere is a grid, not a hierarchy.

Wrong: one step, everywhere

Contract

Taman Sri Indah P2

RM 412,000

Progress

62% claimed

Last claim 14 Aug

Every gap is the same, so the headings float between the two groups they could belong to and the reader has to use the words to work out the structure. Tidy, and silent.

Right: two steps, doing different jobs

RM 412,000

Progress

Last claim 14 Aug

The gap inside a group is a smaller step than the gap between groups, and the space above each heading is larger than the space below it. Measure the rendered gaps and the two comparisons hold, with or without a design system.

Rule L4: depth comes from light, or it is decoration

A shadow that lifts something has an offset and a soft blur, because it stands for a light source the whole screen shares. A zero-offset coloured halo is a glow wearing a shadow's name.

Wrong: a halo, and four elevations from nowhere
Card
Card
Card

No offset, so no light source - the cards glow rather than lift, and the three shadow values were each picked for one component, which is why they do not agree about where the light is.

Right: one light, a small scale of roles
Resting
Raised
Floating

One direction for the offset, blur growing with the distance, and three named roles rather than three numbers. The role is what a component asks for, so a new component cannot invent a fourth shadow.

Rule L6: a region that can grow without limit is bounded and scrolls

Where a list sits beside something else, cap it and give it an expand control, so it cannot stretch the row and strand its neighbour in dead space.

Wrong: the list sets the height of the row

    Three lines of summary, and then a column of nothing, because the neighbour decided how tall this row is.

    Right: bounded, with a way to see the rest

    Activity

      Summary

      The same summary, beside a list that is capped rather than endless. Expand when the list is the thing being read.

      Rule L9: count what the screen asks the user to hold at once

      At any one decision, four is comfortable, five to seven is pushing it, eight is where people start guessing. One primary action, one or two secondary, the rest behind a menu.

      Wrong: nine equal buttons

      Nine choices with equal weight, and the destructive one sits in the row like any other. Nothing here says what this screen is for, so every visit costs the same read.

      Right: one primary, two secondary, the rest in a menu

      The same nine actions, three of them visible. Delete is in the menu and marked, because a destructive action sitting beside Save is a mis-tap waiting to be reported as data loss.

      B - Buttons and their explainers

      The controls a user presses, and how each says what it does.

      Rules B1 and B2: Icon first, label when it fits, always explainable

      Every button leads with an icon; the label is added when there is room and collapses when there is not. A tooltip on hover devices, a tappable [?] on touch, and an accessible name that never depends on either.

      Wrong: text only, or a mystery icon
      no label, no tooltip, no name

      The first row wastes the fastest channel the eye has, which is shape. The second announces only "button", and on a phone there is no hover to reveal what it does.

      Right: icon, label, tooltip, and a [?] for the jargon
      Retention margin

      On touch there is no hover, so tooltips are suppressed and the [?] carries the explanation. That is also why the label only collapses on hover-capable pointers.

      Rule B3: a control that cannot act is a key, not a button

      After making sure it cannot act. If a legend entry, chip, row or badge genuinely has nothing to act on, it loses the border, the hover state and the pressed state, and gains a word saying what it is. Demoting is the second answer, never the first.

      Wrong: looks pressable, does nothing

      Press one. Nothing happens, and now every other button on the page is a maybe. One dead control is enough to make a reader stop trusting the live ones.

      Right: the action first, the key only if there is none

        These filter the table below them, which is the action a legend entry has (C20). Where an entry truly names no subset - a single marker, or a note describing every mark - it renders as the key beneath: flat, unbordered, and labelled as a key.

        • dashed outline = forecast key

        Rule B4: everything that acts answers the pointer, and answers the same way

        Something clickable shows one visible change when the pointer is over it, when it has focus and while it is pressed. A halo drawn around the item is the right default: it takes no layout space, reads on any background and doubles as the focus ring. It is a state cue - it appears on hover, never at rest - so it is not the glow L4 rejects.

        Wrong: three components, three different answers

        Hover each one. Save thickens its border and Export grows, so each moves its own edge out from under the pointer - the hover ends and the item snaps back, a flicker at the border. Share does not answer at all. Three cues, so none of them means "clickable".

        Right: the same ring on everything that acts
        Share Draft

        One halo on the button, the link and the rest, none of them moving. Press Tab and the same ring follows focus. Draft is a label, not a control, so it gets nothing - the ring is only ever a promise that something happens.

        T - Tables and columns

        What every column owes the reader, and what a table must not do twice.

        Standing rule S1: search, sort on every column, a filter on every column

        Search spans all columns, filters narrow one, and the two combine. The result count is shown, and no matches is its own state - not an empty table. Where the row order is itself content, that order is the default and comes back in one click.

        Wrong: a list pretending to be a table
        SiteTypeValue
        Taman Sri Indah P2RenovationRM 412,000
        Bandar Baru Lot 14Fit-outRM 96,500
        Seri Kembangan D3New buildRM 1,240,000
        Puchong Industrial BRenovationRM 233,700

        Nothing sorts, nothing filters, nothing says how many rows there are. It reads fine at four rows, which is the trap: the same component ships with four hundred and the reader has no way in.

        Right: the standard table

        Every header sorts and carries its own filter panel. The count is live, the search and the filters combine, and emptying the result shows a no-matches state rather than a blank body. Try Clear filters: it clears the search and every panel, which is rule A3 - a control that claims to clear, clears.

        Rules T1 and T2: the filter is a panel, and no column is exempt

        Sort, value search, a multi-select list with counts, and a range for numbers - applied live, so there is no Cancel and no OK. Every column gets one, including the computed ones, which means the computed value is materialised onto the row rather than produced inside the cell formatter.

        Wrong: one box, and a column that cannot be filtered
        SiteTypeValueShare no filter
        Taman Sri Indah P2RenovationRM 412,00020.7%
        Bandar Baru Lot 14Fit-outRM 96,5004.8%
        Seri Kembangan D3New buildRM 1,240,00062.3%

        A text box is not a filter: it cannot say "these three types and not the other two", it cannot do a range, and it never tells you what values exist. Share is worse - it is computed inside the formatter, so it cannot be sorted, filtered, searched or exported. The column is a hole in the row.

        Right: a panel per column, computed columns included

        Open Share: it has the same panel as every other column, because the percentage is computed once when the row is built and lives on the row. The panel applies as you tick - nothing waits for an OK, and Escape closes it without undoing what you already chose.

        Rule T3: every column explains itself

        A one-line explainer on every header: a tooltip where there is a pointer, a tappable [?] where there is not. A column whose meaning is obvious to the person who built it is rarely obvious to the person reading it.

        Wrong: abbreviations only the author can read
        SiteProgChgShare
        Taman Sri Indah P262%+420.7%
        Bandar Baru Lot 1491%-24.8%

        Share of what - all revenue, or the rows currently shown? Change since when? Prog by value claimed or by days elapsed? Nobody asks; they guess, and the guess goes into a report.

        Right: hover a header, or tap its [?]

        Same component, same markup: the explainer is a property of the column, so a column cannot be added without one. On a pointer device it is a tooltip on hover and on keyboard focus; on touch the [?] opens a popover, because there is no hover to discover.

        Rule T4: one mechanism per job

        Where a general mechanism exists, a second bespoke one beside it is a defect, not a convenience. A dimension worth filtering is a column, filtered in the panel like every other column.

        Wrong: a toolbar dropdown beside the column filters
        plus a filter panel on the same column
        SiteType also filtered above
        Taman Sri Indah P2Renovation
        Bandar Baru Lot 14Fit-out

        Two filters on one dimension, each with its own state. Set one, and the other still says "All types". Clear filters clears one of them. Every later feature - a saved view, a shared link, a chart that follows the table - now has two places to read and one of them will be forgotten.

        Right: the column filter, and nothing beside it

        The shortcut button does not hold its own state: it sets the Type column's filter, the one the panel drives. Open the panel after pressing it and the tick is already there, because there is only one place the answer lives.

        F - Forms and input

        What a form asks for, when it checks, and what it keeps.

        Rule F1: A required field says so before it is missed

        Mark what is required while the form is still empty, nudge the field when it is left empty on the way out, and on submit stop, count, and land the cursor in the first one.

        Wrong: the form finds out at the end

        Nothing says which of the three matter. Tab straight past Site and the field looks finished. Submit and one generic message appears, with the cursor nowhere near the thing to fix. Press it twice and watch the toast: that is the other half of this section.

        Right: marked, nudged, then blocked

        The asterisk is there before anyone types, and the one field that is not required says so, because marking only the required half leaves the reader guessing about the rest. Leave Site and tab away: it nudges there and then, beside the field, while the form is still on the screen it was on. Submit with two empty: it does not submit, it says how many are missing, and focus lands in the first one.

        Rule F2: a field validates when the user has finished with it

        Check on blur, clear on input, never on keystroke - and take the input type that brings the right keyboard and the right autofill on a phone.

        Wrong: red before the word is finished

        Not a valid email address

        Type one character and it is already wrong, because nobody finishes an address in one keystroke. The field is scolding a person who is doing exactly what was asked. type="text" also means a phone shows the plain keyboard and offers no saved address.

        Right: on blur, cleared on input

        Not a valid email address - it needs an @ and a domain

        Nothing happens while typing. Leave the field empty-handed and the message appears beside it, naming the problem and the fix; start typing again and it goes, without waiting for another exit. type="email" brings the @ keyboard and the saved address on a phone.

        Rule F3: a form is a thing being built, not a thing being submitted

        Work with more than a few fields in it keeps a draft, and a sheet or modal holding unsaved changes asks before it closes.

        Wrong: closing throws the work away

        Type something, close it, open it again. The afternoon is gone and nothing warned anyone. This is the same defect as a tab closing on a half-filled form.

        Right: a draft, and a question on the way out
        no draft

        Typing keeps a draft as you go. Closing with unsaved changes asks first, and the question names what is at stake rather than saying "Are you sure?". Reopen and the text is where you left it.

        Site note

        Site note

        Unsaved: 0 characters. Keep them, or discard?

        Rule F4: a field is the shape of what goes in it

        Content written in lines - a message, a note, an address, a template - gets a field more than one line high that grows as it is typed. A one-line box scrolls sideways instead, so the writer cannot see the sentence they are writing; on a phone, where the keyboard owns half the screen, they see about four words of their own message.

        Wrong: a message in a one-line box

        Preview:

        Type past the width and the start of the message leaves the box. The preview truncates as well, so the one place that could have shown the whole thing shows less of it. Enter cannot make a paragraph here at all - the field has decided the template may not contain one.

        Right: the field grows with the message

        Preview:

        It starts at two lines and grows as you type, to a cap, after which it scrolls inside itself rather than pushing the Save button off the screen. Enter makes a paragraph. The preview wraps, because a preview that truncates the message you just wrote tells you it is wrong when it is not.

        Rule F5: a digit-only field selects its value when it is entered

        If the field accepts nothing but digits - a quantity, a price, a percentage, a year, a phone number - the user is typing a different number, not amending this one. Entering it selects what is there, so the first keystroke replaces it; a second tap inside the selection puts a caret back, because select-all is an offer, not a lock. The trigger is the field's own restriction, so no judgement about intent is needed.

        Wrong: the caret lands wherever you tapped
        no edit yet

        Tap it and type 8. You get 12800, or 81200, or whatever the caret position happened to be - so every edit starts with four backspaces, and on a phone the caret lands where the fingertip did rather than where it was aimed.

        Right: entering it selects it
        no edit yet

        Tap or Tab in and type 8: the field holds 8. Tap again inside the selection and it behaves like an ordinary field, because that tap said "I do want to edit this one". Steppers, where a field has them, adjust the value rather than clearing it.

        Where this does not apply: any field that accepts more than digits - a note, an address, a reference being appended to, a name with a typo. There the typical edit adjusts what is there, and selecting it all means one keystroke destroys it. The test is mechanical rather than a guess about intent: can a letter be typed into this field? Then leave the caret alone.

        Rule F6: a field that holds an amount accepts the arithmetic that produces it

        The number is often the answer to a sum in the user's head - 12 cartons of 24, a price less a discount. The field takes digits, + - * / and brackets, shows the result beside the expression while it is typed, and on commit holds the result. It never runs the text as code, and never touches an identifier or a secret.

        Wrong: the operator is silently dropped
        now 1200

        Type 1200+50. The field refuses the + and keeps the digits, so it holds 120050 - a hundred times the order, with no error anywhere. Refusing arithmetic is not the defect; refusing it silently is.

        Right: the field does the sum
        now 1200
        kept as typed

        Type 12*24, 1200+50 or (3+2)*4 and press Enter. Try 10/3, 10/0 or 5+: the text stays and the chip says why. Select the value and type +50: it extends rather than replaces. The phone field beside it is an identifier - its + and - are part of the number, so nothing is evaluated.

        Rule F7: every field states what it accepts, as one anchored pattern

        Refuse as typed only what can never be valid, normalise what is merely formatting, and check the whole value's shape when the user is done - with a pattern anchored at both ends, written once, and used by the field and the server alike.

        Wrong: strict where it hurts, loose where it matters

        A letters-only name pattern turns away a real surname; a copied email pattern turns away a real address; an unanchored \d{5} lets a sixth digit through; and a phone number is rejected for its dashes. Every verdict here is wrong.

        Right: one anchored pattern, after normalising

        The same four values. The real ones pass; the postcode, which really is a digit too long, is refused with the reason. Edit any field and leave it: the verdict is given on leaving (F2), and the phone is checked with its punctuation removed, not rejected for it.

        Rule F8: typing is never interrupted

        While someone types, nothing the keystrokes cause may replace the field, move its caret, move it on screen or zoom the page. Update what the typing changed, not the region holding the field; put what reacts to typing below it; and keep every field at 16px on a phone.

        Wrong: the whole box redraws on every key

        Type bed. The count and the list sit above the field and shrink as you type, so the field moves up; and the redraw rebuilds the field itself, so focus is gone after the first letter - on a phone the keyboard closes, and the second letter goes nowhere.

        Right: the field stays; only the list changes

          The same data. The field comes first and is never redrawn; the count and the list below it change, and nothing above the field does. Type a word, delete it, type in the middle of it - the caret stays where you put it.

          Rule F9: Enter moves to the next field, and only the last one submits

          In a form of several single-line fields, Enter does what Tab does; only Enter in the last field submits. A form that cannot be undone does not submit on that last Enter: it lands on the button that names what will happen.

          Wrong: Enter submits a half-filled form
          not submitted

          Type a site and press Enter, the way you finish any field. The form is submitted with one field of three filled. This is what an unhandled form does: the browser's own rule is that Enter in any field submits the lot.

          Right: Enter goes on, the last one submits
          not submitted
          nothing deleted

          Enter walks down the fields; Enter in Note submits. The delete form cannot be undone, so its last Enter only lands on Delete site, and a second Enter on the button confirms.

          A - Actions and feedback

          What happens after a press, and how it is reported.

          Standing rule S2: Destructive actions are guarded

          A confirm overlay that names the target, with a clearly labelled cancel. Bulk deletes state the count. Never a bare click, never only window.confirm.

          Wrong: one click from data loss

          The first is unrecoverable on a mis-click. The second names nothing, and cannot be styled, tested, or announced properly.

          Right: named target, clear cancel, undo

          The modal repeats the exact name and the count, Cancel takes focus, Escape cancels, and the success toast carries Undo.

          Rule A1: No silent action

          Every action that changes state, takes time, or can fail tells the user what happened. Progress on the control, outcome in a toast. An action whose result is already visible needs no toast.

          Wrong: silent

          Click it. Did it work? There is no way to tell, and no way to tell success apart from failure.

          The opposite failure is a toast on every click, including sorting a column whose result is already on screen. That is noise, and it trains people to dismiss toasts unread.

          Right: progress on the control, outcome in a toast

          Use Refresh on any chart table below for the full version: spinner in the button, skeleton in the rows, then a toast that alternates success and failure so both are visible.

          Toasts are horizontally centred and anchored to an edge, never a corner. A repeated action replaces its own toast, and the stack caps at three.

          Rule A4: Ship every applicable state

          Loading, empty, error, partial, overflow, no-permission. The state you skip is the one the user hits first.

          Wrong: happy path only
          Invoice #1041RM 2,400
          Invoice #1042RM 980
          Invoice #1043RM 5,120

          While loading: blank. On failure: blank. With no invoices: a container over nothing. All three read to the user as broken.

          Right: four states, one slot

          Loading is a skeleton shaped like the real rows, not a centred spinner. Empty says how to start; error says what happened and what to do next.

          Rule A2: a repeated message counts, it does not restack

          The same toast arriving again keeps the node already on screen, adds a count and restarts its timer. Removing and re-adding it replays the entry animation and reflows the stack, which reads as a broken UI.

          Wrong: a new node every time

          Four presses, four nodes, each re-entering and pushing the others down. Nothing is wrong and the interface looks like it is failing.

          Right: one node, a count, a restarted timer

          The real toast, top or bottom depending on the header toggle. The node does not move: it gains ×2, ×3, and its dismissal timer starts again so the last press still gets its full reading time. An undoable toast is never merged, because each undo belongs to its own action.

          Rule A3: a control that claims to clear, clears everything it claims

          And its label is derived from what it clears. "Clear filters" that leaves a hidden series hidden, a measure unticked and four marks selected is rule A1 in reverse: an action reporting more than it did.

          Wrong: clears the search, keeps the rest
          nothing set

          Set a column filter and a search, then press it. The search goes, the column filter stays, and the button has just told you it cleared the filters. The reader now distrusts the count as well, and they are right to.

          Right: clears all of it, and says how much there was
          nothing set

          The label is derived from what is actually set - it reads Clear 2 filters when two are set, and disables itself when none are, so the control cannot claim work it has none of.

          N - Overlays and navigation

          What claims the screen, what dismisses it, and what survives coming back.

          Rule N1: a modal claims the whole screen, and that is four separate things

          Nothing behind it scrolls, takes focus, or takes a click, and Escape closes it with focus returning to whatever opened it. showModal() gives you three of the four - the background scroll is the one it leaves, and the one that ships.

          Wrong: three of four

          Open it, then scroll with the wheel or swipe. The page behind moves under the dialog, so the thing you came back to is not where you left it - and on a phone the dialog appears to drift off the screen.

          Right: all four

          Same dialog, plus the scroll lock - and the lock pays for the scrollbar it removes, so the page does not jump sideways as it opens. Escape closes it and the focus ring lands back on this button.

          Leaky dialog

          Scroll now. The page behind is still scrolling.

          Sealed dialog

          Scroll now. Nothing behind moves, and nothing behind can be focused or clicked.

          Rule N2: a dropdown closes on choose only when the choice is single

          Multi-select and editable popovers stay open until the user dismisses them. A panel that closes on every tick makes choosing three values three round trips.

          Wrong: closes on every tick

          Tick one and it shuts. Three values means open, tick, reopen, tick, reopen, tick - and each reopen redraws the list, so the ticks you already made have to be found again.

          Right: stays until dismissed

          Every tick applies immediately and the caption above updates, but the panel stays. It closes on Escape, on a click outside, or on the trigger - the three dismissals a reader already knows.

          Rule N2: every panel can be closed, by every route

          Tapping the trigger again closes it, an outside tap closes it, Escape closes it, and on touch there is a visible Done. A trigger that only opens traps the user: a phone has no Escape, and the panel can cover the screen.

          Wrong: the trigger only opens

          Tap Site, then tap it again, and again. It stays open - it was just re-opened each time - and nothing on a phone can close it. Outside taps work only because there is nowhere else on the screen to tap.

          Right: four ways out

          The trigger toggles. Escape closes it and puts focus back on Site. A tap on empty page closes it. Done closes it, at a 44px target on a phone. Ticking a value never closes it: the next tick is the likely next action.

          Rule N4: coming back is a state, not a fresh load

          Where the user was is part of what they were doing: the scroll position, the sort, the filters, what was typed, what was expanded. Going forward and returning restores all of it.

          Wrong: the trip back rebuilds from scratch
          no filter

          Filter it, then take the round trip. The filter is gone, the sort is gone, and the reader is at the top of a list they had already narrowed. The work they did to find the row is exactly the work that was thrown away.

          Right: the view state is read back
          no filter

          Same trip, and the filter, the sort and the search all come back, because they are held in one place the draw reads rather than scattered across the handlers that set them (rule V1). That one place is also what a URL would carry.

          Rule N5, moving: the mark travels, and the ends give a little

          A mark that jumps tells you where you are. A mark that travels also tells you where you came from, which is the thing a reader loses when a whole page changes at once. The travel is a tween: lerp from where it is to where it is going, over a duration, with slow in, a cruise, and slow out in the usual 2:1:2 ratio - at the default total that is 0.2s gathering speed, 0.1s at speed, 0.2s setting down. The flat middle is what makes the distance legible; a curve with no cruise spends the whole journey changing speed and reads as rubbery. At the first and last entry it overshoots by a few pixels and comes back, so the end of the list is felt rather than merely reached.

          Wrong: it teleports, and the ends are silent

          Press the ends, then the middle. The mark is simply somewhere else each time, so a reader who was not watching that exact spot has to find it again - and pressing the last entry twice looks identical to pressing it once, because nothing says there is nothing beyond it.

          Right: it travels, and the ends push back

          The same five destinations. The mark carries the eye between them, and the first and last give by five pixels before settling, which is the whole of the "there is nothing past this" signal - no text, no disabled state, no bounce that anyone would call an animation. Drag the slider: under about 120ms the eye cannot follow the travel and it reads as a jump, past about 700ms the interface feels like it is thinking about it. The ratio holds at every total: the middle is always half of either end.

          What keeps it honest: the indicator reads the pressed element's own box on every frame (rule N5), so it cannot drift out of step with what it marks; it moves on transform only, never on a layout property (rule M1); and where the reader has asked for reduced motion it places itself instantly, because the state change still has to be visible - only the travel goes.

          Y - Type, colour and theming

          The surfaces that get forgotten, and the motion that goes with them.

          Rule Y2: text over a variable background needs a contrast decision

          And there are only two: raise the contrast of the text or the background, or give the text an outline, shadow or backing. The pass mark is a figure - 4.5:1 for body text, 3:1 for large text and for any mark that carries meaning.

          P - Performance and content

          What arrives after the reader has started reading, and content that is bigger than the mock.

          Rule P1: anything that arrives late reserves its space before it arrives

          An image, an embed, a chart, a font: give it its box up front, so nothing already read jumps out from under the pointer.

          Wrong: no box until the content lands

          This sentence is what the reader was reading. Press the button and watch it move.

          Right: the box exists first

          This sentence does not move, because the skeleton is the size of the thing it stands for. A skeleton of the wrong height is a layout shift with extra steps.

          Press both and the measured shift is reported here.

          Rule P2: text is allowed to be longer than the mock

          The text in a row is the part that may shrink, and most layout engines will not shrink it below its content width until told so. Long words break, truncated text stays reachable in full, and a translated label wants roughly a third more room.

          Wrong: the row grows instead of the text shrinking
          Pembinaan Semula Bangunan Pejabat Kompleks Perindustrian

          No min-width: 0, so the text refuses to go below its content width and pushes the button out of the row. The mock said "Site A"; the real record is a fifty-six character name, and half the time it is a German translation of one.

          Right: the text shrinks, the meaning survives
          Pembinaan Semula Bangunan Pejabat Kompleks Perindustrian

          The text is the part allowed to shrink, it truncates visibly, and the full name is still reachable - hover it, or focus it - so truncation hides characters rather than information.

          X - Accessibility and semantics

          The obligations that never scope out.

          Rule X1: Keyboard and semantics from the start

          Real <button> and <a>, visible focus, labels tied to inputs, sensible tab order.

          Wrong: divs pretending
          Save
          Email

          Press Tab: the button is unreachable and Enter does nothing. The label is text beside a box, so clicking it does not focus the field and a screen reader announces an unlabelled input.

          Rule X2: an accessible name states that element's own values

          Never a shared one, and it sits on the element that takes focus. Geometry is shared between rows; values are not. A name built from the scale maximum or the group total is a confident lie told only to the people who cannot see the screen.

          Wrong: every bar claims the same figure

            Rule X3: left and right are content, not layout

            Spacing, alignment and borders are expressed as start and end, so a right-to-left language mirrors the interface instead of breaking it. Icons that mean direction mirror with it; icons that mean a thing do not.

            At a glance

            KPI tiles: the number, the delta, the shape

            The most-used element on any dashboard, and the one most often built as a bare number. A tile answers three questions at once: what is it now, how has it moved, and what has the shape been. The sparkline is not decoration, it is the difference between "down 4 percent" being noise or a trend.

            Each delta names its comparison period. "Up 12 percent" without "against last month" is unreadable. The sparkline shares the time range chosen above, so the tiles and the chart never disagree.

            Vertical charts: a value across periods

            Categories run along the x axis and the measure runs up. Use these when the categories have a natural order, which in practice means time. The eye compares heights against a shared baseline.

            Time series: range, granularity and buckets

            A time chart needs two decisions: how much time and what each mark is. The user picks the range; the granularity is derived from it: the coarsest bucket that keeps the chart under its mark ceiling, currently forty, with a manual override for anyone who wants otherwise. There is no lower bound, because seven days should draw seven bars rather than be padded out to look busier. Rolling and calendar ranges are kept on separate rows because "last 12 months" and "this year" are different numbers that people constantly conflate.

Switch range and the bucket changes with it: 7D gives seven days, 1Y gives thirteen months, 5Y gives quarters, because months over five years would be sixty marks and the ceiling is forty. The last bucket is marked partial when the period has not finished, because a half-finished month otherwise looks like a collapse. Empty buckets render as zero rather than being skipped, which would silently rewrite the axis. The table re-buckets with the chart (C2, C4), so it never shows 1,800 daily rows under a 5-year chart.

              Stacked bar: composition over time

              The pie question and the time question at once. Absolute stacking shows volume and mix together; 100 percent stacking shows mix only, and is the right choice when the totals differ so much that the mix is invisible.

                Candlestick: open, high, low, close

                  Hollow body means close above open, filled means close below. That reads correctly in greyscale and for a red-green colour blind viewer.

                  Waterfall: how we got from A to B

                  Two bars and mental arithmetic is the usual answer to this question. A waterfall shows the bridge itself: each step is a change, the running total carries across, and the closing bar sits on the baseline so it can be read against the opening one.

                    Filtering a waterfall is a trap the other charts do not have: remove a middle step and the remaining bars no longer bridge anything. The running total is recomputed over the visible steps and the chart says so, rather than drawing a bridge that does not add up.

                    Index: comparing things on different scales

                    The honest answer to "compare these two" when one is ten times the size of the other. Rebase every series to 100 at the start and the lines show growth, directly comparable. This is what to reach for instead of a second y axis, which chart rule C1 bans precisely because two independent scales can be tuned to make any two series agree.

                      Scatter: is there a relationship

                      Two measures, one dot each, position carrying both. Use it when the question is whether two things move together. A line would be wrong here: a line implies an order along the x axis that customers do not have.

                                        Rule C5: the highlight follows the mark's own outline

                                        Hover, focus and selection are drawn from the mark's geometry. The browser's default ring on an SVG shape is its bounding box - a rectangle round a curved wedge, covering its neighbours and saying nothing about where the mark's edge is.

                                                    Geographic: where the work is

                                                    A map answers "where", and it is worse than a bar chart at answering "which is biggest", because the eye reads area and area is geography rather than data. Two decisions make the difference between a map that informs and one that decorates: what is shaded, and what the shapes are.

                                                    Tile map: equal tiles, comparable colour

                                                    Real borders make Sarawak shout and Penang whisper, whatever the numbers say. A tile map gives every state the same area, so the colour is the only channel carrying value, and trades away the shape of the country to get there. That is the right trade when the question is about the states rather than about distance. The measure is shaded per site, not as a raw total, because a raw total on a map is mostly a map of where the sites are.

                                                      Mobile shell: the bars that frame everything

                                                      Three bars and a middle. Getting them wrong is the most common mobile defect there is, and it always looks the same: the last item of the list sits underneath the bottom bar, permanently unreachable, because the content was given the whole viewport and the bar was laid on top of it.

                                                      Rules L8 and N3: the standard frame, and who arranges the bar

                                                      Four or five slots plus a menu holding every destination, not just the leftovers - it is the menu being complete that makes a five slot bar safe (N3). Where the destination set is large and which destinations matter differs by user, the arrangement becomes a preference, edited by dragging, with a keyboard route to the same thing; where every user has the same job, fixed slots plus the complete menu is the whole rule. And the frame itself (L8): sticky top bar, scrolling middle, optional action toolbar, bottom navigation. The bars are siblings of the content, not layers over it, so the middle is the only thing that scrolls and nothing can slide underneath. Where an overlay bar is unavoidable, the content's bottom padding is measured from the bar at runtime, never typed in as a number that the next design change will invalidate.
